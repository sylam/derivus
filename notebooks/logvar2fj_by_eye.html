LogVar2FJ by Eye

A vol surface has about five things you can see in it. This model has eight numbers and a curve. Here is which number owns which feature, and — the part that does the work — over which maturities each one is allowed to act.

First, delete one axis

The at-the-money level is not a parameter

The ξ strip is bootstrapped inside every objective evaluation, one segment per at-the-money expiry, so every candidate parameter set reprices the at-the-money term structure exactly — the notebook's misses are ~1e-11 relative. Whatever the eight parameters do, they cannot move the spine of the surface.

So when you look at a surface and ask what the parameters are, the level is already answered. What is left to fit is the shape across strike, and how that shape changes with maturity.

The organising axis

Two factors, two clocks

The log-variance is driven by two Ornstein-Uhlenbeck factors, and the only thing that separates them is how fast they forget. Kappa_S 6 gives the fast factor a two-month memory; Kappa_L 0.5 gives the slow one about two years. That is the whole reason there are two.

Read the chart as a budget: how much variance each factor has accumulated by maturity T, measured from the base date, which is exactly the Var(ℓ+s) the engine derives L* with. The fast factor fills up and stops. Past about six months, every new unit of uncertainty is the slow factor's — and on this ladder the slow factor is a pinned class default, not a fitted number.

The short end

The residual is a stopwatch

The part of a return leverage does not explain spends a variance clock A = c·∫ξ and carries a normal-inverse-Gaussian increment on it. Alpha and Beta are not readable on their own — they are per unit of clock. What you can read is what they do to the distribution, and the two quantities below are exact NIG cumulants.

Both die as the clock runs: skewness as 1/√A, excess kurtosis as 1/A. At one month this residual is visibly non-Gaussian; by a year it is a rounding. So Alpha and Beta are short-dated smile parameters and nothing else — which is exactly why a ladder of one-month-and-longer vanillas struggles to pin them, and why notebook 2 goes looking for a one-month forward-start.

The thing you actually see

Skew is a product, and all of it decays

Spot skew comes from the leverage products Rho_S × Sigma_S and Rho_L × Sigma_L: a variance shock correlated with the return tilts the smile. Each factor's contribution decays on its own clock, the fast one first. The residual's skew decays too, as 1/√A.

That is the whole story of this fit's residual. The chart below normalises the model's total skew to 100% at the first expiry, so only the shape matters — and beside it is what this fixture's ladder asks for, which is a 90–110 skew of −6.02 vol points at every expiry, identical to five decimals. Nothing the model can do produces a flat line.

What is exact here. The ξ figures are the engine's own output. The variance budget, the corner, the stationary and 5-year spreads, and the residual's skewness, excess kurtosis and α·δA shape are closed forms taken from the engine's own spellings — at the fitted values the shape reads 1.177 at one month and 18.77 at a year, which is what the notebook's report prints.

What is a shape. The skew panel plots ρσ·g(κT) with g(x) = (1−(1−e−x)/x)/x, the first-order term structure of leverage skew. It is normalised, so it says which factor owns which maturity and how fast the total decays — not how many vol points. Replacing that layer with real smiles means a parameter sweep through the pricer, one job per rung.
